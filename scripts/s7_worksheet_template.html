1. Executive Summary & Professional Aviation Relevance

Teaching a complex technical subject to others is one of the most effective ways to solidify language mastery and professional communicative presence. In airside operations, civil aviation oversight, and flight operations management, future ENAC graduates are regularly required to deliver safety briefings, train frontline ground teams, explain complex EASA regulatory updates, and facilitate collaborative decision-making across international teams.

Developing the confidence to give direct spoken instructions, facilitate interactive peer discussions, and think on your feet in English transforms passive technical knowledge into active leadership communication. In this session, student pods lead 30-minute interactive workshops, practicing instruction delivery, checking understanding, table coaching, and leading plenary debriefs.

Part 1: Entry Diagnostic Spot-Check — Spoken Facilitation & Instruction Techniques

Before teams take the floor, test your spoken facilitation knowledge with these 4 quick-fire spot-check questions on instruction checking, active monitoring, pacing, and debriefing.

Q1: Instruction Checking Questions Clarity Check

What is an ICQ, and why is asking "Do you understand?" ineffective?

Model Answer: An ICQ verifies specific task parameters (e.g., "Are you working in pairs or alone?", "How many minutes do you have?"). Asking "Do you understand?" produces reflexive nods without confirming comprehension.
Q2: Active Table Coaching Group Monitoring

What should student instructors do while classmates work in pairs?

Model Answer: Instructors circulate actively among tables to listen to discussions, clarify ambiguities, prompt quieter students with guiding questions, and maintain an English-only atmosphere without interrupting unnecessarily.
Q3: Timebox Management Pacing Control

How should a team manage the 30-minute timebox if an activity runs over?

Model Answer: Issue a clear spoken time warning ("Two minutes remaining, please finalize conclusions"), accelerate transition to debrief, and nominate specific teams for highlights rather than hearing every pair.
Q4: Plenary Debrief Quality Class Synthesis

What makes a plenary debrief effective in technical English?

Model Answer: An effective debrief synthesizes peer discoveries, elicits contrasting solutions, highlights accurately deployed aerodrome phraseology, and gives immediate actionable feedback rather than simply reading slides.

Part 2: The 30-Minute Flipped Lesson Architecture (4 Stages)

Zero rigid minute breakdowns. Each team determines its internal dwell time and discussion pacing according to peer engagement and communicative flow:

STAGE 1 OPENING PHASE

Welcome & Context Hook

Introduce your team of instructors and set the real-world aerodrome scenario. Hook peer curiosity with a compelling problem:

  • State clear, plain-language session goals (e.g. "Today we evaluate Runway 14L friction loss").
  • Display a visual exhibit (photo, diagram, or NOTAM extract).
  • Ask an opening engagement question to activate background knowledge.
STAGE 2 CORE ACTIVITY

Instructions & Spoken Task

Deliver step-by-step instructions in plain B2 English before breaking classmates into pairs or small groups:

  • Use numbered imperatives ("Step 1: identify the hazard. Step 2: draft mitigation").
  • Ask 2 to 3 targeted ICQs before saying "begin".
  • Designate partner roles (e.g. Aerodrome Inspector vs. Airside Operations Manager).
STAGE 3 ACTIVE COACHING

Circulating & Table Support

All team instructors split across the room to support, monitor, and coach student pairs:

  • Circulate between tables; avoid standing together at the front whiteboard.
  • Prompt quieter learners with guiding questions (e.g. "What does Part-ADR require?").
  • Gently re-channel French discussions back into professional English.
STAGE 4 DEBRIEF PHASE

Plenary Review & Debrief

Bring the whole class together to compare solutions, synthesize insights, and celebrate communicative success:

  • Nominate specific tables to share their primary safety recommendations.
  • Recap the core technical distinction (e.g. Level 1 immediate closure vs Level 2 CAP).
  • Thank classmates for active participation and invite peer questions.

PART 3: SPOKEN FACILITATION TOOLKIT • LANGUAGE CLINIC SECTION: INSTRUCTIONAL SPOKEN FLUENCY

Spoken Facilitation Toolkit — Phrases for Student Instructors

Deploy these practical, natural B2 phrases during your 30-minute delivery to direct the room, check understanding, coach pairs, and lead the debrief:

📢 1. Opening Hook & Instructions
  • "Good morning everyone. Today our team is putting you in the shoes of airport safety managers..."
  • "Our primary objective by the end of this 30-minute session is to agree on..."
  • "Please work in pairs with the person sitting next to you."
  • "Step one is to examine the chart. Step two is to identify the non-compliance."
❓ 2. Instruction Checking Questions (ICQs)
  • "Before we start, who can explain what you need to do in step 1?"
  • "Are you working individually or in pairs?"
  • "What should your team have written down when time is up?"
  • "You have 8 minutes for this phase. Any questions before we start?"
👥 3. Circulating & Table Coaching
  • "How are things progressing over here? Any questions on the regulatory rule?"
  • "What does EASA Part-ADR state about temporary obstacle exemptions?"
  • "Try using 'provided that' to express the conditional safety measure."
  • "Two minutes remaining, everyone. Please start wrapping up your notes."
🎯 4. Plenary Debrief & Eliciting
  • "Let us hear from Team B. What root cause did your group identify?"
  • "Did any other team reach a different conclusion for this audit finding?"
  • "To recap, the key technical distinction between Level 1 and Level 2 is..."
  • "Great participation everyone. Thank you for your active engagement."

Language Clinic: Spoken Facilitation & Instruction Drills

Transform hesitant, vague teacher talk into direct, confident spoken instructions:

Facilitation Scenario ❌ Hesitant / Vague Spoken English ✅ Plain B2 Facilitation (Direct & Confident)
1. Directing Pairwork & Steps
Step sequencing		 "Maybe you can look at the paper and see what is wrong, do it with someone." "Please work in pairs. Step one: examine the obstacle limitation surface on page 3. Step two: circle the antenna penetration. You have four minutes."
2. Formulating ICQs
Verifying comprehension		 "Do you understand the activity?" "Before we begin: Sarah, how many root causes does your pair need to write? Exactly, three. And David, how much time do you have? Six minutes."
3. Table Coaching & Hints
Guiding stuck students		 "Why are you not talking? You must write the answer." "How is it going over here? Look back at EASA Part-ADR.OR.D.005: does this containment failure pose an immediate hazard to flight operations, or can it be managed within 30 days?"
4. English Immersion Reminder
Encouraging English		 "No French, English please!" "Remember everyone, 100% English only. If you need the word for 'brise-souffle', it is 'jet blast deflector'. Keep the discussion in English!"
5. Transition to Debrief
Nominating speakers		 "Okay time is up. Who wants to talk?" "Time is up, everyone. Let us hear from Team C. Alexandre, what was the primary systemic cause your group agreed on?"

ACTIVITY 5: SAFETY CHECK • SYSTEMIC RISK DECISION TABLE SECTION: AERODROME SAFETY OVERSIGHT

Activity 5: Safety Check — Systemic Communication & Briefing Failures

Review the 4 questions in the Safety Check table to analyze how ambiguous spoken instructions, unstandardized terminology, and poor briefings create operational hazards on the airfield:

Simple Safety Question Agreed Class Answer & Regulatory Standard (EASA Part-ADR.OR.D.015)
1. What is the systemic communication problem? Airfield operations rely heavily on spoken communication during shift handovers, contractor briefings, and ramp coordination. Ambiguous instructions, inconsistent phraseology, or passive listening create immediate latent hazards.
2. What could happen if uncorrected? Misunderstood runway closure limits, unclear vehicle routing, or unconfirmed safety exemptions can trigger runway incursions, ground collisions, and unauthorized access to active movement areas.
3. How do we fix it? Enforce standardized B2 plain English instruction protocols: require explicit readbacks, ask targeted Instruction Checking Questions (ICQs) before work begins, and verify physical understanding rather than assuming compliance.
4. Directorate Operational Decision Mandate that all aerodrome supervisors, team leaders, and safety instructors complete certified spoken briefing assessments under EASA Part-ADR.OR.D.015, enforcing standardized interactive briefing techniques across all airside teams.

Standardized Spoken Briefing Protocols (Airfield Operations)

Four non-negotiable operational rules for delivering technical briefings on active movement areas:

1. Standardized Plain Terminology

Use established ICAO/EASA aerodrome terms (e.g. holding point, apron stand, displaced threshold). Avoid colloquialisms or local airfield slang that international drivers or contractors might misunderstand.

2. Mandatory Readback Verification

Every critical safety instruction—especially vehicle routing, active runway crossing permits, and work zone boundaries—requires an explicit, verbatim verbal readback before movement commences.

3. Direct Instruction Checking (ICQs)

Verify understanding with direct questions: "Which taxiway is closed?", "What is the maximum vehicle speed on Apron 2?". Never rely on passive head-nodding.

4. Closed-Loop Shift Handovers

Outgoing and incoming airside supervisors must conduct a face-to-face spoken debriefing, verifying open NOTAMs, active work in progress, and unserviceable airfield ground lighting (AGL).
